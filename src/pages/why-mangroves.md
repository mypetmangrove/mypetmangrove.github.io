    heading: Natural climate solutions
    body: >-
      Mangroves are one of the planet's most powerful natural climate solutions. Found along tropical
      and subtropical coastlines, these salt-tolerant trees form dense forests that anchor sediment,
      protect shorelines, and fuel marine biodiversity. Yet their most remarkable function lies unseen,
      beneath the surface, in the carbon-rich soils they stabilize.
    image: /images/site/mangrove-shore.jpg
    image_alt: Dense mangrove trees along the water's edge
  - type: split
    background: mist
    image_side: left
    heading: Blue carbon
    body: >-
      Mangrove ecosystems store **up to four times more carbon per hectare** than other tropical
      forests, rainforests included, locking away "blue carbon" in their roots and waterlogged
      sediments for the long term. When mangroves are lost to development or pollution, this stored
      carbon is released back into the atmosphere, amplifying climate change.
    callout: "Source: Donato et al., Nature Geoscience (2011)."
    image: /images/site/underwater-roots.jpg
    image_alt: Mangrove roots seen from underwater
  - type: split

    heading: Biodiversity engines and climate shields
    body: >-
      Mangrove roots are nurseries for snook, snapper, tarpon, red drum, oysters, and shrimp. They
      anchor sediment and soften waves and storm surge, and they trap sediment and excess nutrients
      before runoff reaches seagrass and reefs. Restoring mangroves doesn't just sequester carbon. It
      safeguards livelihoods, fisheries, and water quality.
    image: /images/site/nursery-sharks.jpg
    image_alt: Young sharks swimming between mangrove roots

  - type: cards
    background: white
    heading: Florida's three native mangroves
    intro: >-
      From the water's edge to higher ground. All three sprout seedlings while still on the parent
      tree, and their propagules ride the tide: free, native baby trees.
    items:
      - title: Red mangrove
        body: |-
          *Rhizophora mangle*

          Closest to the water. Arching prop roots earn it the name "walking tree."
      - title: Black mangrove
        body: |-
          *Avicennia germinans*

          A little higher. Finger-like pneumatophores poke up so the roots can breathe.
      - title: White mangrove
        body: |-
          *Laguncularia racemosa*

          Highest and farthest inland. No visible aerial roots; you know it by its leaves.

    heading: Why they need us
    body: |-
      In Florida, mangroves face:

      - Coastal development and hardened shorelines
      - Habitat alteration, including mosquito impoundments along the lagoon
      - Pollution and nutrient runoff
      - Boat damage
      - Climate stress: hard freezes, and a northward push into salt marsh
